Challenge

Origin Energy’s sales teams were managing complex, high-volume workflows across Salesforce, but the system was slow, manual and disjointed. Lead management, data entry and handoffs between teams took significant effort, and the experience for both staff and customers reflected that.

The scale of it came out in the research. Team members estimated they spent sixty to seventy percent of their time on manual admin — time they’d rather have spent with customers and brokers. Around a third of pricing was being done by hand. An operations team that used to absorb the admin no longer existed, so it had landed on the sales team on top of everything else. And the system failed quietly: one participant updated what they thought was a single account, updated several, and only found out when a privacy breach notification arrived.

Origin engaged Designit to redesign these workflows and map out a better future state.

Solution

A research-led redesign of Salesforce workflows across Business Direct and Major Customer teams — rebuilding lead capture, triaging, qualification and nurture around clearer handoffs and less manual effort, refined through concept testing with stakeholders.

My Design & Approach

As UX Designer on a six-week sprint, I worked alongside Angel, a Service Designer, and Mika, who was then second-in-charge to our Managing Director, focused on the discovery and future-state design phases. I supported Angel on interviews with sales team members across Business Direct and Major Customers, all with five to ten years at Origin, covering their current process, what mattered most to them, their pain points and what an ideal process would look like.

We mapped the sales process end to end as seven steps, from getting a lead through to ongoing relationship management, and rated each step for how relevant it was and how severe the problems were. That surfaced the extra steps nobody had documented — accepting an offer, the pre and post relationship work — and made it clear where the pain concentrated rather than where people assumed it was.

The future state we designed automated the front of that process: routing a lead to the right sales team based on consumption data, scoring it on value, win rate and due date, and categorising whether it needed manual or automatic pricing rather than leaving someone to work that out.

Design Challenge

Design Challenge: Designing for a Team That Had Already Given Up on the System

The interviews turned up workarounds everywhere. One person kept their customers, their NMIs and correspondence details in a paper notebook. Another used Tableau’s account lookup because finding an account in Velocity took too long. People batched twenty offers at a time rather than working one through end to end. On major customers, nine times out of ten they’d skip the offer process altogether and send an educated quote instead.

Those weren’t people misusing the system. They were rational responses to a tool that cost more than it gave back — and they’d been told before that a new tool would fix it, then watched it add work instead. Consultants had already been through once without changing anything.

So the workarounds became the brief rather than something to design away. Each one marked a place where the system was slower than a notebook, and the future state had to be measurably faster than the workaround, not just better than the official process nobody was following.

Result

The future-state design was projected to save 600+ hours per week across key sales activities. It also improved the experience for customers and brokers interacting with the sales process — less lag, clearer communication, and fewer handoff gaps.

What I Learnt

Six weeks is a tight timeline for that scale of transformation, so prioritisation was everything. I learned to focus research on where the effort-to-impact ratio was highest — not trying to solve everything, but being very clear about which workflows, if redesigned, would free up the most time and create the most downstream value. That discipline has stayed with me.
