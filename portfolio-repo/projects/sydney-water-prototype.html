Sydney Water: Three Journeys Through Procurement

Three people, three routes through the same broken system, built to land with an audience that signs business cases.

Challenge

Wipro holds a long-term contract with Sydney Water and was working to renew and strengthen it through an RFP response. As part of that pitch, Wipro needed a way to show Sydney Water exactly where their SAP-based employee procurement experience was falling short — and to do it in a way that would land with an audience who sign off on business cases, not interfaces.

Solution

Three narrative journeys, one for each kind of person who uses the system, each moving from their current experience to a future one and ending in what the change is worth to the business.

My Design & Approach

I built this solo, drawing on research previous service designers had done on an earlier Sydney Water project, along with conversations with Wipro SMEs embedded at Sydney Water.

Rather than one generic walkthrough, I split it by user type, because the system fails different people in different ways. I wrote the three personas myself and grounded what each of them needed in those earlier research findings. Sam, an occasional user, logs in for a small job and gets the same dashboard as everyone else, so a five-minute task turns into hunting for the right form. Alex, a new starter, finds contract search overwhelming and falls back on a free-text request assuming it’s the simpler path — it isn’t, it’s the one that triggers manual work for the purchasing team and drags the request out. Kiran processes high volume and loses sight of everything the moment it’s submitted, spending two to three hours a day chasing approvals by phone and email.

Each journey runs the same way: the current experience annotated in red over the real screens, the future experience annotated in green, then a panel translating it into the terms the pitch was actually being judged on — approval cycle times, manual intervention, how quickly a new employee becomes productive.

Design Challenge

Design Challenge: Grounding Pain Points in Real Evidence

The risk with a persuasive prototype is inventing pain points that feel dramatic but aren’t real. I worked from research service designers had already done at Sydney Water and from Wipro SMEs who spent their days inside the environment, so the friction I surfaced reflected what people who knew the system had already found rather than what would make the best slide.

Alex’s story is the clearest example. A free-text request isn’t an obvious pain point from the outside — it looks like a feature, and someone guessing at problems would never land on it. It only shows up as a problem when you know what happens downstream.

Result

The prototype gave Wipro something concrete and tangible to bring into the RFP conversation, and landed well enough that the scope expanded into a much bigger piece of work: reimagining the full intake-to-procurement flow (see the iBuy case study).

What I Learnt

A deck full of pain points is easy to nod along to and then forget. Following one named person from frustration to resolution made people stop on the exact screen where it broke down. Splitting it three ways mattered as much — a single generic user would have flattened three different problems into one, and it was the differences between them that made the case.
