Challenge

Wipro had been Sydney Water’s primary vendor for years and was the systems integrator that built iBuy — a large bespoke layer sitting on top of SAP. A major contract renewal was coming, Wipro was mid-RFP, and they weren’t the clear favourite to keep the full scope. So they funded a short design engagement, four weeks from February, to show Sydney Water what a design-led uplift to procurement could look like before the decision was made.

The constraint was the interesting part. Everything we proposed had to be aligned with solution architecture and genuinely feasible. A compelling vision that couldn’t survive delivery would have been worse than nothing, because the audience were the technical people who’d have to build it.

Solution

A future-state vision for iBuy, built in SAP Fiori and grounded in how procurement actually works at Sydney Water. Delivered as a self-guided prototype with five paths people could choose between — shopping supplier catalogues, shopping pre-approved contracts, requesting a new item, tracking a request, and searching the contract hub — rather than one linear demo.

My Design & Approach

I worked with Lauren, Lead User Experience Designer at Designit, over four weeks: map the current state from existing walkthroughs, consolidate what needed to change from the earlier research, design the future-state flow, then position it as something Sydney Water could react to. Before Lauren joined the project I was taking the work directly to Mika, Designit’s Australian Managing Director, and Sheree, our Design Director, for approval.

We started by mapping the three ways anyone actually buys something. Free-text, when there’s no contract and no known supplier. Contract-based, through the internal catalogue. And punch-out, which sends you to an external supplier’s own site and back again. I built out the current-state flows for each with real screenshots, plus the approval flow and the Manage PR and Manage PO paths, and dug into what SAP gives you out of the box so we knew what we were adding versus what already existed.

Laying the three side by side made the core problem obvious. Free-text is the only path that requires purchase requisition approval and pulls the procurement team in manually. The other two auto-convert to a purchase order with no procurement involvement at all. And free-text is exactly what people fall back on when they can’t work out which supplier or contract applies — so the path that feels like the shortcut is the one that creates the most work for everyone.

We had almost no access to real users, so feasibility came from Wipro’s SMEs who work inside the platform daily, tested in short sessions rather than a formal research programme.

We built it to be explored rather than presented. Sydney Water would be circulating it internally to people with very different jobs and very different tolerance for procurement jargon, so a linear walkthrough would have meant everyone sitting through four scenarios to reach the one that mattered to them. Instead each story stands alone, with the reasoning written beside the screen — what changed, and why — so it holds up without anyone in the room to narrate it. That’s also where a lot of the language work landed: replacing the old category and sub-category entry with one plain-English entry point and in-context help, and putting a “what is a contract?” explainer right where someone would need it.

Design Challenges

Design Challenge 1: Following the Task, Not the Persona

The earlier RFP prototype was structured around three personas, and my instinct was to carry that forward. The team pushed back, and the structure changed to following a single task as it moves between different people — requester, approver, procurement — rather than making personas the organising principle.

That turned out to be right for this audience. The pitch wasn’t about who these users are; it was about where a request gets stuck as it passes between hands. Personas would have told three parallel stories. Following the task told one, and made the handoffs the point.

Design Challenge 2: Designing Around a Fixed System of Record

Ariba remained the system of record for supplier catalogues, and we couldn’t design that away. Instead we made iBuy the guided front door, handing off to Ariba for catalogue browsing and detail, then returning to iBuy for a single consistent checkout no matter which of the three paths someone had taken.

Result

The RFP outcome isn’t something I have visibility on. What the engagement produced was a future-state vision that had to satisfy two audiences at once — credible enough for the solution architects who’d have to build it, and clear enough for people who don’t know the system to follow on their own. Grounded in the three real procurement paths, the existing research, and what the platform can actually do, rather than an idealised journey nobody could build.

What I Learnt

Ariba wasn’t going anywhere, so the job wasn’t to replace it. It was to design iBuy as the layer that made Ariba feel like one coherent journey instead of a system you get dumped into halfway through. Same lesson iFly taught me at Air New Zealand: sometimes the work is building a good front door onto a system you don’t get to touch.
